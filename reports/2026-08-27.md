## Preliminary SWOT (listing-only, not diligence)

### #1 — SW Houston Specialty Grocery — $1.0M
- **S:** Nearly 30-year operating history (since 1997) in a defined ethnic-grocery niche (Russian/Eastern European/international) — long tenure suggests a loyal, sticky customer base rather than foot-traffic-dependent retail; SBA loan eligibility flagged directly by the seller/broker, which usually means the broker has already run preliminary numbers past a lender.
- **W:** Grocery retail carries thin structural margins and heavy inventory/perishables risk; a specialty/ethnic customer base is a strength until it's a concentration risk — if the surrounding neighborhood's demographics shift, the core customer base can erode gradually and quietly.
- **O:** Import/specialty grocery is a category e-commerce hasn't fully commoditized (fresh and bulk imported goods travel poorly), giving some insulation from online competition; nearly three decades of supplier relationships could support private-label or expanded import lines.
- **T:** Single-location retail is exposed to a lease renewal or rent-increase risk not visible from the listing; Houston's Eastern European/international population base and any recent immigration-pattern shifts would materially affect a niche like this — worth checking independently rather than assuming stability continues.

### #2 — Absentee Owned Therapy Practice — $1.1M
- **S:** Explicitly marketed as absentee-run, meaning clinical and administrative staff already carry day-to-day operations without owner involvement — a meaningfully different risk profile than most owner-operator small businesses in this price band; established since 2006 (two decades), and healthcare services demand tends to be less cyclical than retail or discretionary trades.
- **W:** "Absentee-owned" cuts both ways: if it runs fine without an owner today, the acquirer is buying a management team's competence as much as the underlying practice, and that team's retention post-sale is unconfirmed; healthcare practices often carry licensure, payer-credentialing, or clinician-specific relationships that don't automatically transfer to a new owner.
- **O:** Multi-location or referral-network expansion is common in therapy/behavioral health roll-ups if the existing management infrastructure can support it; recurring-visit models (vs. one-time transactional services) tend to have more predictable cash flow.
- **T:** Reimbursement-rate risk (insurance/Medicare/Medicaid policy shifts) is a sector-wide exposure not mentioned in the listing; clinician turnover or departure of a key therapist could disproportionately affect revenue in a smaller practice — worth confirming clinician count and tenure during diligence.

### #3 — Self-Serve Auto Wash w/ Real Estate — $750K
- **S:** Real estate is included in the asking price, which is unusual in this budget band and changes the deal's risk profile — you're buying a hard asset with resale/refinance optionality, not just a cash-flow stream; recently renovated (2023–2024) on a business established in 1986, suggesting the physical plant is in good condition despite the low current cash flow.
- **W:** The disclosed $45,000 cash flow is the headline problem — on a $650,000 loan (after $100K down) at 9.75%/10yr, debt service alone would run roughly $102,000/year, more than double the disclosed cash flow. This is not a marginal case; it's a straightforward math failure unless the real-estate value materially changes the deal structure (e.g., a much larger down payment sized to the land value, or valuing this as a real-estate purchase with a struggling operating business attached, not an operating-business purchase).
- **O:** If the real estate itself carries most of the $750K value, this could still work as a different kind of deal — buy the land/building, operate or lease out the wash bays — but that reframes it entirely away from an SBA business-acquisition loan sized against SDE.
- **T:** Self-serve car washes are low-differentiation and rate-sensitive to utility costs (water/sewer); the listing's own $45K cash flow figure may already reflect real, structural earnings weakness rather than an anomaly — take the seller's number at face value rather than assuming diligence will find materially more.
