## Preliminary SWOT (listing-only, not diligence)

### #1 — SW Houston Specialty Grocery — $1.0M
- **S:** Nearly 30-year operating history (since 1997) in a defined ethnic-grocery niche (Russian/Eastern European/international) — long tenure suggests a loyal, sticky customer base rather than foot-traffic-dependent retail; SBA loan eligibility flagged directly by the seller/broker, which usually means the broker has already run preliminary numbers past a lender.
- **W:** Grocery retail carries thin structural margins and heavy inventory/perishables risk; a specialty/ethnic customer base is a strength until it's a concentration risk — if the surrounding neighborhood's demographics shift, the core customer base can erode gradually and quietly.
- **O:** Import/specialty grocery is a category e-commerce hasn't fully commoditized (fresh and bulk imported goods travel poorly), giving some insulation from online competition; nearly three decades of supplier relationships could support private-label or expanded import lines.
- **T:** Single-location retail is exposed to a lease renewal or rent-increase risk not visible from the listing; Houston's Eastern European/international population base and any recent immigration-pattern shifts would materially affect a niche like this — worth checking independently rather than assuming stability continues.

### #2 — Decorated Motorcycle Parts Retailer (ecommerce) — $925K
- **S:** ~20 years of operating history in a defined enthusiast niche, positioned as a market leader with a global customer base — longevity in ecommerce (a space with high turnover) is itself a signal; second-highest DSCR of the six, decent cushion.
- **W:** Listing description is thin on channel mix (owned site vs. Amazon/marketplace dependence) and supplier concentration — for an ecommerce acquisition those two facts drive most of the real risk, and neither is disclosed here; "decorated" motorcycle parts is a narrow sub-niche, which cuts customer acquisition cost but caps total addressable market.
- **O:** Two decades of SEO/brand equity and an existing customer list are hard to replicate — a new owner could extend into adjacent enthusiast categories (parts for other vehicle types) using the same audience.
- **T:** Ecommerce-specific risks apply directly: marketplace policy changes (Amazon/eBay fee or policy shifts), import-cost/tariff exposure on physical parts, and the platform-dependency question above; a niche this specific can also be vulnerable to a single well-funded competitor entering.

### #3 — Digital Traffic Intermediary Business — $1.15M
- **S:** Fully remote/relocatable — removes geographic constraints on Craig's search entirely; both SDE ($330K) and EBITDA ($250K) are disclosed, which is more financial transparency than most listings in this price band offer, and named enterprise clients suggest some contract stability rather than pure transactional traffic.
- **W:** The $80K gap between SDE and EBITDA implies real owner compensation or addbacks that need scrutiny — that gap is exactly where "SDE" claims get generous; "traffic intermediary" business models (lead-gen/affiliate) are inherently a pass-through, meaning the business owns the relationship with neither the traffic source nor necessarily the end client exclusively.
- **O:** Home-services lead-gen is a large, still-growing category as more contractors buy leads online rather than door-knock; a remote-operable asset like this could be run alongside other holdings without relocation.
- **T:** This category lives and dies by platform algorithm changes (Google Ads/organic, ad-network policy) and by client contract non-renewal — both are largely outside a new owner's control; "motivated seller" language in the listing is worth asking about directly rather than assuming it's neutral.

### #4 — Absentee Owned Therapy Practice — $1.1M
- **S:** Explicitly marketed as absentee-run, meaning clinical and administrative staff already carry day-to-day operations without owner involvement — a meaningfully different risk profile than most owner-operator small businesses in this price band; established since 2006 (two decades), and healthcare services demand tends to be less cyclical than retail or discretionary trades.
- **W:** "Absentee-owned" cuts both ways: if it runs fine without an owner today, the acquirer is buying a management team's competence as much as the underlying practice, and that team's retention post-sale is unconfirmed; healthcare practices often carry licensure, payer-credentialing, or clinician-specific relationships that don't automatically transfer to a new owner.
- **O:** Multi-location or referral-network expansion is common in therapy/behavioral health roll-ups if the existing management infrastructure can support it; recurring-visit models (vs. one-time transactional services) tend to have more predictable cash flow.
- **T:** Reimbursement-rate risk (insurance/Medicare/Medicaid policy shifts) is a sector-wide exposure not mentioned in the listing; clinician turnover or departure of a key therapist could disproportionately affect revenue in a smaller practice — worth confirming clinician count and tenure during diligence.

### #5 — 22-Year Specialty Apparel Brand — $1.25M (price unconfirmed, see flag above)
- **S:** Two decades of operating history and stated category leadership in a specialty apparel/intimacy-products niche — highest disclosed cash flow of the six ($343K), and a long tenure suggests real brand equity and a repeat-customer base rather than a fad product.
- **W:** Beyond the unresolved price conflict, no direct listing page could be located — everything here rests on search-snippet marketing language ("market dominance") that is a seller/broker claim, not a verified fact; no detail on wholesale vs. DTC revenue mix or channel concentration.
- **O:** A 22-year brand in a niche category likely has underused wholesale, licensing, or line-extension potential if it hasn't been actively pursued by the current owner.
- **T:** Specialty apparel is exposed to fashion-cycle and input-cost risk; a brand this age can also carry an aging customer base or a dated online presence — worth checking traffic and social data independently. Given the inability to verify this listing at all beyond conflicting search snippets, treat this as the lowest-confidence candidate in the report regardless of its DSCR.

### #6 — Self-Serve Auto Wash w/ Real Estate — $750K
- **S:** Real estate is included in the asking price, which is unusual in this budget band and changes the deal's risk profile — you're buying a hard asset with resale/refinance optionality, not just a cash-flow stream; recently renovated (2023–2024) on a business established in 1986, suggesting the physical plant is in good condition despite the low current cash flow.
- **W:** The disclosed $45,000 cash flow is the headline problem — on a $650,000 loan (after $100K down) at 9.75%/10yr, debt service alone would run roughly $102,000/year, more than double the disclosed cash flow. This is not a marginal case; it's a straightforward math failure unless the real-estate value materially changes the deal structure (e.g., a much larger down payment sized to the land value, or valuing this as a real-estate purchase with a struggling operating business attached, not an operating-business purchase).
- **O:** If the real estate itself carries most of the $750K value, this could still work as a different kind of deal — buy the land/building, operate or lease out the wash bays — but that reframes it entirely away from an SBA business-acquisition loan sized against SDE.
- **T:** Self-serve car washes are low-differentiation and rate-sensitive to utility costs (water/sewer); the listing's own $45K cash flow figure may already reflect real, structural earnings weakness rather than an anomaly — take the seller's number at face value rather than assuming diligence will find materially more.

### #7 — Remote Asset-Light Energy Solutions Business — $1.0M
- **S:** Asset-light model (no office, no warehouse, no vehicles, no inventory) is unusual and attractive at this price point — most of the balance-sheet risk typical in a services business is stripped out; national/remote footprint means it isn't tied to any one local market's economy; highest DSCR of the nine candidates in this report; seller financing offered, which often signals the seller has confidence in continued performance.
- **W:** Asset-light also means the "business" is largely a subcontractor network and customer relationships — if the current owner personally manages those subcontractor relationships, that's a key-person risk not visible in the listing; margin (SDE $393,792 on $1,972,612 sales, ~20%) is thinner than the dollar cash-flow figure alone suggests.
- **O:** LED retrofit, EV charging, and water-efficiency work all sit inside a growing commercial-real-estate decarbonization/cost-reduction trend — tailwinds look durable rather than fad-driven; asset-light structure would let a new owner scale without heavy capital reinvestment.
- **T:** Commercial energy-efficiency incentives (utility rebates, tax credits) can be policy-dependent and subject to change; being "remote" cuts both ways — no local relationships or reputation moat protecting against a new competitor undercutting on price.

### #8 — Established Epoxy and Resinous Flooring Business — $800K
- **S:** Explicitly positioned as suitable for an owner-operator *or* semi-absentee buyer, with systems, trained crews, and supplier relationships already in place — that's a meaningfully lower operational-risk profile than a typical trades business; customer acquisition already runs mostly through referrals/repeat business rather than paid ads, which usually means healthier unit economics; independently cross-listed via a business broker's own site, adding some confidence this is a real, current listing.
- **W:** No location disclosed in what I could pull — that's a real gap for a trades business, where local market conditions and licensing requirements matter a lot; "trained crews" is a strength only if key crew members stay through a change of ownership, which isn't confirmed.
- **O:** Mix of recurring residential work and larger commercial/industrial contracts gives two different growth levers — a new owner could lean harder into whichever segment they're better positioned to sell.
- **T:** Epoxy/resinous flooring is a skilled-labor trade — regional contractor supply and licensing requirements vary, and without a location this can't be assessed; commercial/industrial contract revenue can be lumpy compared to steady residential work.

### #9 — Trash Hauling Business — $1.0M
- **S:** Waste hauling is a genuinely recession-resistant category — trash collection isn't discretionary, which supports the "recession-resistant" framing in the listing rather than it being pure sales copy; established operations and existing customer relationships reduce the up-front sales burden a new owner would otherwise face.
- **W:** DSCR here is only 1.12 — marginal by CLAUDE.md's own bar, and a lender may push back or require a larger down payment; cash flow ($157,765) is low relative to the $1.0M asking price (a roughly 6.3x SDE multiple), which for an asset-heavy hauling business is worth scrutinizing — check what trucks/equipment are actually included versus separately valued.
- **O:** Waste/recycling is a fragmented industry nationally, with room for route density improvements or add-on acquisitions if the new owner wants to grow past the current single-operation footprint.
- **T:** Equipment-heavy businesses like hauling carry real maintenance/replacement capex that doesn't always show up cleanly in a seller's discretionary earnings figure — a truck due for replacement soon could materially change the true economics; no location disclosed, so local disposal-facility/tipping-fee costs (a major cost driver in this industry) can't be assessed from the listing alone.
